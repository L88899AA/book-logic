# Harbor delivery study

## Capacity and delivery

The fictional harbor bought a faster unloading crane. Unloading time fell from four hours to two hours, but trucks still waited for an average of six hours. The single exit gate, not the crane, limited the number of completed deliveries.

The operations team had reported unloaded containers as its success measure. Customers measured containers delivered to their warehouses. Improving the first measure did not automatically improve the second.

This case does not show that faster cranes are useless. After the exit gate was expanded, the faster crane became useful. The benefit depended on the capacity of the whole route.

## Cash and purchasing power

The fictional harbor increased its maintenance budget from 100 tokens to 120 tokens. During the same period, the price of a replacement part rose from 10 tokens to 15 tokens. The old budget bought ten parts; the new budget bought eight.

The finance team therefore separated the nominal budget from the quantity of parts it could purchase. The example compares one specified part and period, not every cost in the harbor.

## Information and planning

For a confidential timetable redesign, managers excluded the night-shift operators. The draft schedule omitted the nightly safety inspection and could not be followed as written.

The operators were later invited to review the schedule under the same confidentiality rules. They added the inspection window. The lesson in this fictional case is about access to relevant operational knowledge, not a claim that confidentiality always makes planning fail.
